2 Final Check — Cube Scale Factor & Decimal Addition
Problem 1
Two cubes are shown. Cube A has sides with length 2 millimeters. Cube B is 5 times as wide as Cube A. How many Cube A’s can fit inside Cube B?
AnswerC (125)
Why: Cube A’s volume is 2×2×2=8 cubic mm. Cube B’s side is 5×2=10 mm, so its volume is 10×10×10=1,000 cubic mm. 1,000÷8=125. The shortcut: cube the scale factor (5³=125) — 150 comes from a surface-area-style calculation (5×5×6), which counts faces, not how many solid cubes fit inside. Kept as a final check — this was the single most historically stubborn gap found (6+ confirmed instances), even though fresh numbers on it in redo-set-12 were correct.
Problem 2
What is the value of the expression 6.5 + 1.25?
AnswerD (7.75)
Why: Line up the decimal points before adding: write 6.5 as 6.50 so the tenths line up with tenths and the hundredths line up with hundredths. 6.50 + 1.25 = 7.75.

Problem 4

AnswerC (more than 1½ and less than 2 liters)
Why: 2.2−0.5=1.7 liters left. 1.7 is more than 1.5 and less than 2, which is choice C. Choice A (less than ½ liter) would mean almost the whole carton is gone — a big underestimate, since less than a quarter of the milk was actually poured out. Problem 6

AnswerC (in between $110 and $115)
Why: Round each price: shirt ≈$34, socks ≈3×$3=$9, shoes ≈$67. Total ≈$34+$9+$67=$110. The exact total is $34.49+3($2.99)+$67.49=$110.95, which falls in the $110–115 bucket. Picking $100–105 is a full bucket too low — worth double-checking that each rounded piece actually adds up before choosing a range. From the book, page 128.

Problem 8

Problem 10
Ms. Giroux had 112 milk cartons in a refrigerator in the school cafeteria. She puts 31 more cases of milk cartons in the refrigerator. Each case contained 18 cartons. The expression 112+(31×18) represents the exact number of milk cartons in the refrigerator now. Which of these is the best estimate of the number of milk cartons in the refrigerator in the school cafeteria?
AnswerC (700)
Why: Round 31 to 30 and 18 to 20: 30×20=600, then add the 112 back in to get about 700. More precisely, 31×18=558, plus 112=670, which rounds to 700, not 600. Rounding both factors down is fine, but forgetting to add the 112 back in (or rounding it away) undershoots into the wrong bucket. From the 9/13 online session.

Problem 18
The total price of three apples and one banana is $3.50. If the cost of each apple is twice the cost of each banana, which equation can be used to find the cost of each banana, b?
AnswerB (7b=3.50)
Why: If a banana costs b, each apple costs 2b. Three apples cost 3(2b)=6b. Total: 6b+b=7b=3.50. Picking 4b=3.50 suggests the “apple costs TWICE as much” multiplier got dropped or misapplied — always translate each item’s cost in terms of the SAME variable before combining them.
4 Sticky Gap — Multi-Step Unit Conversion
Problem 19
How many inches are in 3 yards? (1 yard = 3 feet and 1 foot = 12 inches)
AnswerD (108 inches)
Why: 3 yards = 3×3 = 9 feet. 9 feet = 9×12 = 108 inches. Picking 36 is only 1 yard’s worth (1×3×12=36) — the conversion was done correctly for ONE yard, but never multiplied by the 3 yards actually asked for. Always finish the whole chain: convert the UNIT first, then scale by the actual quantity.
Problem 20
How many pints are in one and a half gallons? (1 gallon = 4 quarts, 1 quart = 2 pints)
AnswerB (12 pints)
Why: 1½ gallons = 1.5×4 = 6 quarts. 6 quarts = 6×2 = 12 pints. Picking 8 suggests only 1 gallon’s worth of conversion was completed (4 quarts×2=8 pints), dropping the extra half-gallon partway through the two-step chain.
Problem 21
How many milligrams are in 0.06 kilograms?
AnswerC (60,000 milligrams)
Why: 0.06 kg = 60 g (kg→g is ×1,000). Then 60 g = 60,000 mg (g→mg is ×1,000 again). Picking 60 stops after only the FIRST step of this two-step conversion (kg→g), never continuing on to milligrams.
Problem 22
Which of the following is equal to 16 pints? (1 gallon = 4 quarts, 1 quart = 2 pints, 1 pint = 2 cups, 1 cup = 8 fluid ounces)
AnswerA (2 gallons)
Why: 16 pints ÷ 2 = 8 quarts. 8 quarts ÷ 4 = 2 gallons. Picking “4 quarts” is only 8 pints (4×2=8), not 16 — half the correct amount. With several conversion facts given at once, it helps to convert everything down to the SAME smallest unit (like pints) before comparing choices.
3 Sticky Gap — Decimal/Fraction to Percent: Which Way the Point Moves
Problem 23
What is 0.5 as a percent?
AnswerD (50%)
Why: 0.5 as a percent means moving the decimal point 2 places right: 0.5=50%. Picking 0.5% keeps the decimal in the same place instead of shifting it — the exact reverse-direction version of the "off by a factor of 10" mistake, but for a decimal-to-percent conversion instead of a unit conversion.
Problem 24
What is 2.62 as a percent?
AnswerD (262%)
Why: 2.62 as a percent: shift the decimal 2 places right, 2.62=262%. Picking 26.2% shifts only 1 place instead of 2 — check the full 2-place shift every time, not just a partial one.
Problem 25

AnswerD (375%)
Why: 3¾=3.75, and 3.75 as a percent is 375%. Picking 334% suggests the fraction ¾ wasn’t converted to .75 cleanly first — convert the fraction to a decimal as its own explicit step before shifting the decimal point.

Problem 27
What percent of 30 is 60?
AnswerD (200%)
Why: 60÷30=2=200%. Picking 50% suggests the fraction got built backwards (30÷60 instead of 60÷30) — when the number you’re comparing TO is smaller than the number itself, the percent must be over 100%, which is a quick sanity check against this exact trap.
Problem 28

AnswerA (20)
Why: 30 is 150% of a number means 30=1.5×x, so x=30÷1.5=20. Picking 45 (=30×1.5) multiplies instead of divides — the classic reverse-percent trap: when the question gives you the RESULT and the percent and asks for the ORIGINAL number, you divide by the percent, you don’t multiply.

AnswerA (The value of Y is always ¼ times the value of the length.)
Why: Y=¼×length means Y is always ¼ times the length — that IS the given relationship, read directly. Picking ¾ is the complement of ¼ (1−¼=¾), the same "answers the complement instead of the target" pattern as the probability problem above.
Problem 31
The base of a triangle is 6 meters (m). Which statement correctly compares the value of A to the base and height of the triangle? (A=½×base×height)
AnswerB (The value of A is always 3 times the value of the height.)
Why: A=½×base×height, and base=6, so A=½×6×height=3×height — A is always 3 times the height. Picking "can be the same as the height" isn’t a fixed multiplying relationship at all — once the base is fixed at a specific number, plug it in and simplify to find the ACTUAL constant multiplier before picking a choice.
